Product decision record · KWI · Work in development

Unifying four transfer workflows without forcing one way of working.

This case study describes my product decisions and process using public-safe context. It does not claim launch outcomes for work that is still in development.

My role

Product strategy, discovery, workflow design, prototype validation, requirements, and phased rollout planning

Scope

One transfer-management experience replacing four fragmented legacy workflows

Users

Store teams creating, shipping, receiving, and reconciling inventory transfers

Status

In development; no migration or error-reduction outcome is claimed

01 · The product problem

The visible problem was fragmentation. The harder problem was variance.

Four legacy tools divided a single business process across transfer creation, bulk updates, receiving, and box-level receiving. Consolidation could reduce navigation and training burden, but a superficial merge would fail because stores do not all receive inventory in the same way.

The product needed to support scan-led work, picklists, item-level discrepancies, box-level receiving, partial receipts, quantity variances, permissions, and controls around what could change after a transfer was booked.

02 · The decision

Build one product around shared states, with controlled paths for different operating models.

I chose a unified transfer model rather than recreating four screens inside a new shell. Shared states and validations provide consistency; configurable receiving modes and exception paths preserve the operating differences that matter.

Standardize

Transfer state, core validation, permissions, discrepancy visibility, and the path from creation through receiving.

Keep flexible

Item- versus box-level receiving, partial receiving, variance thresholds, entry methods, and selected post-booking controls.

Make explicit

Active-store checks, inventory availability, identifier validity, duplicate dates, invalid box states, and required comments.

03 · How I reduced uncertainty

Prototype the exceptions, not only the happy path.

I mapped the end-to-end journeys, then used prototypes to test how rules and exceptions appeared during real tasks. Codex and Claude Code helped me create and refine workflow prototypes and explore edge cases faster; the business rules and decisions remained grounded in discovery and cross-functional review.

  1. Discover

    Map current workflows, user roles, operational constraints, and unresolved client differences.

  2. Define

    Document states, validations, permissions, discrepancies, and API expectations.

  3. Prototype

    Make the workflow tangible enough for users, Engineering, and QA to challenge assumptions.

  4. Phase

    Sequence capability and migration so adoption risk can be managed rather than deferred.

04 · What success will require

A modern interface is not the outcome. A trusted migration is.

Because the product remains in development, I do not publish migration or error-reduction results. The working success model focuses on completion time, exception and variance rates, receiving accuracy, support volume, adoption by workflow, and retirement of the legacy paths.

The broader lesson: modernization is a product change-management problem. The quality of the new experience matters, but so do parity, explainable exceptions, migration sequencing, and evidence that users can safely leave the old workflow.
